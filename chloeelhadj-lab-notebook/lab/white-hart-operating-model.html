The White Hart Operating Model

Guests experience a hotel as one seamless stay. Behind the scenes, that experience depends on hundreds of small decisions shared across the front desk, housekeeping, maintenance, the restaurant, and management. This project examines the information system behind those decisions — and explores where AI can strengthen organizational memory without replacing human judgment.

Why the White Hart?

The White Hart is a boutique inn in Salisbury, Connecticut. Its relatively small scale makes the organization understandable while still containing the operational complexity of a much larger hotel. Because departments interact constantly, it provides an ideal setting for studying how information—not hierarchy—coordinates daily operations.

The Information Problem

Traditional organizational charts describe reporting relationships. Boutique hotels, however, operate through information relationships: observations, preferences, requests, and decisions constantly moving between people and departments.

The Front Desk emerges not as the center of authority, but as the organization's primary coordination hub. It is where fragmented information becomes coordinated action.

The challenge is that much of this knowledge remains informal. It lives in conversations, personal notes, and employee experience — making it valuable but difficult to retrieve, transfer, or preserve.

The AI Opportunity

This project does not treat AI as a replacement for hospitality expertise. Instead, it explores AI as an organizational memory layer: a tool for capturing, structuring, and retrieving information that already exists inside the organization.

The goal is not to automate judgment. It is to reduce information loss — helping employees access context at the moment decisions need to be made.

From Concept to Prototype

The following prototypes explore how an information-centered operating model could become useful in practice: first by surfacing operational context for managers, then by capturing the informal knowledge that makes boutique hospitality distinctive.

The Morning View surfaces information that already exists across the hotel. The next question is how the organization captures knowledge before it disappears — especially the observations, patterns, and context that employees carry informally.

Behind this interface is a consolidated information layer: the systems, records, and employee notes that make operational context available in one place.

The Daily Operations Interpreter explores a narrow role for AI: organizing free-form operational notes into structured categories that can be retrieved and shared. It does not replace employee judgment or make decisions; it helps preserve the knowledge that would otherwise remain trapped in individual memory.

Once information becomes structured, its value depends on how it moves through the organization. Following one operational issue reveals where context is preserved, where it is lost, and where human judgment remains essential.

Large systems become understandable by following one piece of information. This workflow traces a single maintenance issue from guest request to resolution, revealing each handoff, decision point, and opportunity for information loss.

The most valuable information inside a boutique hotel often exists nowhere in software. It lives in experienced employees: recurring guests, room quirks, trusted vendors, seasonal patterns, and unwritten service standards. This knowledge is both the hotel's competitive advantage and its greatest operational risk: valuable when shared, vulnerable when it remains trapped inside individual experience.

Key Insights

01 — The Front Desk is the hotel's primary coordination hub.

02 — Most operational friction occurs during information handoffs.

03 — Institutional knowledge is operational infrastructure.

04 — AI is most valuable when retrieving and coordinating information, not replacing human judgment.

Reflection

I began this project expecting to design an AI tool. Instead, I ended up studying organizations. The most important insight wasn't where AI fits — it was how much of a hotel's success depends on information that is shared through people rather than software. Understanding that operating system became more interesting than automating it.
